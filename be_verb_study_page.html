The verb “to be”

Intro: what is “be”?

Go one step at a time. Each step builds on the one before.

1
Verbs show an action or a state
Action: run, eat, write. State: know, like, own, seem. A state verb tells how something is. It is not something you do.
2
“Be” is the most basic state verb
It tells how someone or something is: She is a doctor. He is kind. They are at home. There is a problem.
3
“Be” is irregular
Its forms change a lot: be, am / is / are, was / were, been, being. The next tab shows each form.
4
“Be” has two jobs
Main verb: it describes a state. She is happy. Helping verb: it helps another verb. She is eating. The apple is eaten. To tell them apart, look at the word after “be”.
5
A state verb does not usually take V4 (-ing)
✗ I am being here. → ✓ I am here. The exception is behaviour: He is being silly means he is acting silly now.

What “be” means as a main verb

As a main verb, “be” is a linking verb. It links the subject to information about it.

There are five kinds of information: exists, happens, located, identity and description.

A. Exists

Says that someone or something is there. Use “there is” for one and “there are” for more than one.

  • There is a rabbit inside.
  • There is nothing in the fridge.
  • There are two cats here.

B. Happens

Says when an event takes place.

  • The party is tonight.
  • The meeting is at five o’clock.
  • The test was yesterday.

C. Located

Says where a person or thing is.

  • She is at school.
  • She is home.
  • The food is on the table.
  • The meeting is down the hall.

D. Identity

Gives a name, a job, a role, or where someone is from (origin).

  • She is Lara and this is Bob.
  • He is a singer.
  • He is not a singer.
  • Alex is from Ireland.
  • Sonia is Spanish.

E. Description

Tells what someone or something is like: colour, age, feelings, weather, price.

  • Our dog is black.
  • I am tired.
  • My father is 50.
  • It is sunny today.

Be in tenses

“Be” shows up in tenses in two ways: as the main verb, and as a helping verb in the continuous tenses. The passive has its own tab.

Pronouns cover all three persons: 1st (I, we), 2nd (you), 3rd (he, she, it, they). Every noun is 3rd person: one noun = he / she / it, more than one noun = they. After “will”, the verb is always “be”, for every subject.

Being (V4) shows behaviour and is used only with words like rude or polite. You cannot say “I am being here”. Been (V3) comes after have / has / had and joins the past to now. It does not mean “finished”.

Part 2 · Helping verb

“Be” as a helping verb: the 6 continuous tenses

Sometimes “be” is not the main verb. It comes before a V4 verb and helps it show an action in progress.

FormulaSubject + be form + V4

TenseStructureExample
1Present ContinuousS + am / is / are + V4 + OShe is writing a letter.
2Present Perfect ContinuousS + have / has been + V4 + OShe has been writing a letter.
3Past ContinuousS + was / were + V4 + OShe was writing a letter.
4Past Perfect ContinuousS + had been + V4 + OShe had been writing a letter.
5Future ContinuousS + will be + V4 + OShe will be writing a letter.

Quick pattern to remember
Simple→ BE + V3
Continuous→ BE + BEING + V3
Perfect→ HAVE + BEEN + V3
Future→ WILL + BE + V3

V1 = write  ·  V2 = wrote  ·  V3 = written

Ways “be” is used

From simple to advanced. Each way shows the pattern first, then examples.

Level 1 · Basic

Be after a modal verb

After will, can, might, must, should

After a modal verb, the verb is always “be”. It never changes.

Subject + modal + be + …

  • Dinner will be ready soon.
  • The kids might be at the park.
  • You must be careful.

Be as a link verb

A link verb joins the subject to information about it. This is the main-verb use from Tab 1. It is also a state verb: it describes how something is.

After “be”What it tellsExample
a noun phraseidentityMy aunt is a nurse.
an adjectivedescriptionThe soup is very hot.
a place phraselocatedThe keys are on the table.
from + a placeorigin (identity)They are from Spain.

More basic uses

There is / there are

Says that something exists or is in a place. “There” is only a placeholder subject. The real subject comes after “be”, and “be” matches it.

There + is / are + subject + place

  • There is a cat on the sofa.
  • There are two apples in the bowl.

“The cat is there” is different: here “there” means “in that place”.

Be in commands

When you tell someone what to do, the verb is plain “be”. There is no subject.

Be + adjective  /  Don’t be + adjective

  • Be quiet.
  • Don’t be late.

Be in questions and negatives

“Be” does not need “do”. In a question it moves in front of the subject. In a negative, “not” goes after it.

  • Statement: She is at home.
  • Question: Is she at home?
  • Negative: She is not (isn’t) at home.
  • Short answer: Yes, she is.
Level 2 · Intermediate

Be as a helping verb

Be + V4: continuous

Shows an action in progress. See Be in tenses.

Subject + be form + V4

  • We were walking home.
  • It has been raining all day.

Be + V3: passive

The subject receives the action. See Be in the passive.

Subject + be form + V3

  • The window was broken.
  • These toys are made of wood.

Be with “to”: plans and near future

Be going to

A plan you already decided, or something you can see coming. “Be” is the helping verb and V1 follows “to”.

Subject + am / is / are + going to + V1

  • I am going to cook tonight.
  • Look at those clouds! It is going to rain.

Be about to

Something will happen in the next moment.

Subject + am / is / are + about to + V1

  • The bus is about to leave.
  • We are about to start.

Be able to, used to, supposed to

“Be” comes before an adjective-like word, then “to”. Each one has its own meaning.

  • She is able to swim. (can)
  • He is used to waking up early. (it is normal for him; V4 follows)
  • You are supposed to knock. (the rule says so)

“Be used to” is different from “used to” for a past habit.

Level 3 · Advanced

Be after a noun, or after “it”

Noun + be + that-clause

Names a problem, an idea or a fact, then explains it with a clause.

Noun + be form + that + clause

  • The problem is that I lost my pencil.
  • The good thing is that nobody was hurt.

Common nouns: problem, idea, fact, hope, reason, rule, truth, trouble.

Noun + be + to + V1

Names a plan or a wish, then says what it is.

Noun + be form + to + V1

  • My plan is to read every night.
  • Her wish was to see the sea.

Common nouns: plan, aim, wish, hope, idea, job, promise, decision.

It + be + adjective + clause

Comments on something that happened or is true. The clause starts with that or a question word (what, who, how).

It + be form + adjective + that / wh- clause

  • It is lucky that we found the bag.
  • It is not clear who won.
  • It was amazing how fast she ran.

Common adjectives: clear, true, lucky, strange, sad, odd, possible, obvious.

Special structures

Imaginary situations: “were”

For wishes and things that are not real, use “were” with every subject, even I, he, she and it.

  • If I were you, I would take that deal.
  • He acts as if he were the boss.

In everyday talk, “If I was…” is common. In careful writing, use “were”.

Formal “be” after “that”

After words like important, essential or suggest, formal writing uses plain “be” for every subject.

  • It is important that he be on time.
  • The teacher suggests that she be early.

This is rare in everyday talk. People say “he should be” or “he is” instead.
