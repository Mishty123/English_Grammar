Indefinite Pronouns

An indefinite pronoun refers to people, things or amounts without saying exactly which one. Examples: someone, nothing, many, all.

Four things to know about indefinite pronouns

What it does

It refers to a person, a thing or an amount without saying exactly which one. someone is a person we do not name or do not know.

What stays the same

It has no subject form, no object form and no gender. “Someone called.” “I called someone.” Only ownership changes the word: someone’s.

The verb

Some words take a singular verb (everyone is). Some take a plural verb (both are). Some follow the noun (all of the water is, all of the boys are).

Pronoun or determiner

Alone, it is a pronoun: “Many came.” Before a noun, it is a determiner: “Many students came.” This page is about the pronoun job.

someone = the indefinite pronoun students = the noun it stands for or comes before is = the verb

Subject of a verb

Someone called. Everybody is here.

The verb is always singular (V5): everyone is, nobody was, something has. This includes everyone and everybody, which mean many people.

Object of a verb

I saw someone. She told nobody. He knows everything.

The word does not change. someone is the same as a subject and as an object.

After a preposition

with someone, for everyone, about nothing

We talked about everything.

Ownership

Someone’s phone rang. It is nobody’s fault. Everyone’s favorite song is on.

Add ’s to the word. Nothing else changes.

An adjective goes after

I want something cold. She is someone special. There is nothing new.

The adjective comes after the word, not before. Wrong: “a cold something.”

else

Ask someone else. I want something else. Everyone else left.
This is someone else’s bag.

else means other. For ownership, ’s goes after else, not after the first word. Wrong: “someone’s else.”

Followed by to + verb

I need something to eat. She has nothing to wear. We need someone to help.

to + the base verb tells what the thing or person is for.

One word or two

Everyone passed. (all the people)
Every one of the boys passed. (each single boy)
Anyone can come. (no matter who)
Any one of the boys can come. (one boy out of the group)

Before of, write two words. Without of, write one word.

Some, any and no some- · any- · no-

The same choice works for someone / anyone / no one, something / anything / nothing and some / any / none.

The uses, one by one

Positive: some-

I saw someone in the garden. Somebody is at the door. I want something sweet.

It means a person or thing that exists, but is not named or not known.

Negative: not + any-

I did not see anyone. She does not want anything. We do not know anybody here.

not and any- go together.

Negative: no-

I saw no one. She wants nothing. We know nobody here.

Same meaning as not + any-. Use one negative word only.

No- starts a negative sentence

Nobody called. Nothing happened.
Wrong: Anyone did not call. Right: Nobody called.

Do not begin a negative sentence with any- + not. Use no-.

One negative only

Wrong: I did not see nobody.
Right: I did not see anybody. / I saw nobody.

Standard English uses one negative word in a clause. Two negatives cancel each other. Some dialects use them, but exams do not.

Questions: any-

Did anyone call? Is there anything to eat? Do you know anybody here?

In a plain question, any- asks whether there is even one.

Offers and requests: some-

Would you like something to eat? Can I ask you something?

You expect the answer yes, so use some-.

Any- in a positive sentence

Anyone can learn. You can ask anybody. Take anything you like.

It means it does not matter who or what.

Hidden negatives

She left without saying anything. I hardly know anyone. He never tells anybody.

without, hardly, barely, never, rarely, seldom already make the meaning negative, so use any-. Do not add not: wrong “I do not hardly know anyone.”

Some- at the start of a negative sentence

Someone did not lock the door.

It means one person, not known, did not lock it. It does not mean nobody locked it.

Some, any and none on their own

I have some. I do not have any. I have none. Do you have any?

The noun is left out because it is clear. Before a noun, none becomes no: “I have no books” = “I have none.”

Short answers

“Who called?” “Nobody.” “What did you buy?” “Nothing.”

A short answer that means no one or nothing uses no-.

Amounts many, much, few, little, several, fewer, less

Choose by what you are counting: things you can count, or things you cannot.

manymuchmany friends · much time

several—several books

You can count books (one book, two books), so books is countable. You cannot count water, time, money or information, so they are uncountable.

The uses, one by one

many: things you count

Many were late. How many came? Too many left.

Plural verb (V1). many stands for many people or things.

much: amounts you cannot count

Much has changed. How much is it? I do not have much.

Singular verb (V5). In an everyday positive sentence, use a lot: “I have a lot,” not “I have much.”

few and a few

Few came. (almost nobody)
A few came. (some people)

few means almost none, and it sounds negative. a few means some, and it sounds positive. Both take a plural verb.

little and a little

Little is known. (almost nothing)
A little is enough. (some)

Same idea as few and a few, for amounts you cannot count. Singular verb.

fewer and less

Fewer came than we expected.
Less was said than we expected.

fewer is for things you count (plural verb). less is for amounts (singular verb).

several

Several agreed. Several of them were late.

More than two, but not many. Plural verb.

Amount word + of

many of them · few of us · much of the time · several of the students

After of, use the object pronoun (them, us, you, him, her, it) or the / my / these + noun. Wrong: “many of they.” The verb still follows the amount word: “Many of the students are late.” “Much of the time was wasted.”

a lot and lots

I have a lot. A lot of them came. Lots are left.

Everyday English uses these in positive sentences. The verb follows the noun after of. See the All, most, none tab.

All, most, some, none and others all, most, some, any, more, half, enough, none

These words name a part or all of a group or an amount. The verb follows the noun after of, or the noun the word stands for.

Rule. Find the noun. A plural noun takes a plural verb (V1: are, have). An uncountable or singular noun takes a singular verb (V5: is, has).

The uses, one by one

How to choose the verb

  1. Find the noun after of, or the noun the word replaces.
  2. Plural noun: use a plural verb.
  3. Uncountable or singular noun: use a singular verb.

Some of the cake is left.
Some of the cakes are left.

all: the whole group

All of us agree. All are welcome. We all agree.

After of, use the object form (us, them). When all follows the subject, the subject form stays (we all).

all: everything, or the only thing

All is well. All I want is peace.

Here all is singular. All I want means the only thing I want.

most and more

Most agree. More are coming.

most means more than half. more means an extra number or amount. With no noun, the meaning of the sentence tells you if it is plural.

some

Some like tea; others like coffee.
I have some.

some is a part, not all and not none. For the choice between some and any, see the Some, any, no tab.

none

None of the boys are here. None of the water is left.

none means not one, or not any. With a plural noun, both a plural verb (everyday English) and a singular verb (formal) are correct. With an uncountable noun, use a singular verb.

none or no one

None of the students passed.
No one called while you were out.

none points to a particular group or amount, usually with of. no one means nobody, people in general. no one is only for people.

Both, each, either and neither both, each, either, neither

These words pick from a group. Three of them are about two items. each works for two or more.

The uses, one by one

both

Both are ready. Both of them are ready. They both left.

both looks at the two together. Plural verb. After of, use the object form (both of us). After a subject pronoun, both follows it (we both).

either

Either is fine. Either of the answers is correct.
“Tea or coffee?” “Either.”

either looks at one of the two. It does not matter which. Singular verb. In speech, a plural verb after either of the + plural noun is also heard. Use the singular in exams.

neither

Neither is ready. Neither of the boys is here.

neither means both are not. It is already negative, so do not add not. Wrong: “Neither of them did not come.” Singular verb in exams.

each

Each has a pen. Each of the girls has a pen.

each looks at one at a time. Singular verb, even when of + a plural noun follows.

each after the subject

They each have a pen. We each know our job.

When each comes after a plural subject, the verb agrees with the subject (they, we), not with each.

each at the end

The tickets cost $2 each.

Here each means for one.

either…or and neither…nor

Either Tom or the girls are late.
Neither she nor I am ready.

The verb matches the nearer subject. The Agreement tab of the Personal Pronouns page has the full table.

either and neither as adverbs

I do not like it either. Neither do I.

These are adverbs, not pronouns. They mean also not.

The uses, one by one

one: one from a group

One of the boys is late. One of them has my pen.

The verb matches one, not the plural noun after of. Wrong: “One of the boys are late.”

one and ones: standing in for a noun

I want the red one. Which shoes? The red ones.

one replaces a singular noun, and ones replaces a plural noun. It saves repeating the noun.

one: people in general (formal)

One should always check one’s work.

Formal. Everyday English uses you or we. The forms are one, one’s, oneself. See the Personal Pronouns page.

another

I do not like this shirt. Show me another. Would you like another?

another = an + other, so it is singular. It means one more of the same kind, or a different one. With a noun: another book.

the other

I have two pens. One is blue; the other is red.

Use it only when the group has two. After one, the one that is left is the other.

others and the others

Some like tea; others like coffee.
Two students stayed. The others left.

others means some different ones, not all. the others means all the rest. Both are plural.

Matching patterns

One is red; the other is blue. (exactly two things)
Some are red; others are blue. (not all of them are listed)
Some are red; the others are blue. (all the rest are blue)

other before a noun

Other people agreed. We met the other day.

other before a noun never ends in s. Wrong: “others people.” others is the pronoun and stands alone.

Agreement

Check three things: the verb, the pronoun that refers back, and the question tag.

The verb

Singular verb (V5)

Everyone is here. Nobody was late. Each has a book. Something smells good.

Compounds, each, either, neither, one, much, little and less. Wrong: “Everyone are here.”

Plural verb (V1)

Both are here. Few know. Many have left. Several were late.

both, few, fewer, many, several, others.

The verb follows the noun

All of the water is gone.
All of the boys are here.

For all, any, more, most, none, some, enough, half. See the All, most, none tab.

Word + of + plural noun

One of the boys is late. Each of the girls has a pen. Neither of the answers is right.

For one, each, either, neither, the verb matches the first word, not the plural noun after of.

The pronoun that refers back

their

Everyone should bring their book. Somebody left their umbrella.

Modern English uses their after everyone, someone, anyone, nobody, each. Older exam style writes his or her. Choose one style and keep it in the sentence.

Plural words

Both of the boys lost their books. Many left their coats.

Things: it and its

Something fell, and it broke. Everything has its place.

something, anything, nothing, everything use it and its.

Reflexive

Everyone enjoyed themselves. Nobody hurt themselves.

Match their: use themselves. Older style: himself or herself.

Which word? Four questions

Ask them in order.

  1. Does it name a place (somewhere, anywhere)? It is an adverb, not a pronoun.
  2. Is a noun right after it? It is a determiner (many students). No noun, or of follows? It is a pronoun.
  3. What kind of sentence? Positive: some-. Negative: not any- or no-. Question: any-. Offer: some-.
  4. Which verb? Compounds, each, either, neither, one, much, little: singular. both, few, many, several, others: plural. all, most, some, none, more, half: follows the noun.
