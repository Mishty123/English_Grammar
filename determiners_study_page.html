Determiners

A determiner comes before a noun and says which one, whose, how many or how much. Examples: a, the, this, my, some, two.

Four things to know about determiners

What it does

It limits a noun. It tells which one (the, this), whose (my, Sara’s) or how many and how much (some, two, much).

Where it goes

First in the noun phrase, before any adjective. the old house, my two sisters, some cold water.

How many

A noun usually takes one main determiner. Say my book or the book. Do not say “the my book.”

It must fit the noun

Some determiners go only with singular nouns (a, this, each). Some go only with plural nouns (these, many, few). Some go only with uncountable nouns (much, little).

the = the determiner book = the noun it goes with old = an adjective, which comes after the determiner

Determiner, adjective or pronoun?

Look at what comes right after the word, and at where the word can stand.

The the test

the red book (an adjective, so the fits before it)
“the my book” (does not work, so my is a determiner)

An adjective can follow the or a. A determiner cannot, because it already does the same job.

Adjectives can stack, determiners cannot

a big old red car  /  “a the this car” is wrong

Use several adjectives if you like. Use one main determiner.

Adjectives can follow a verb

The car is red. (adjective after is)
“The car is my.” (wrong) → The car is mine.

A determiner never stands after be. The matching pronoun does.

Test. Is there a noun right after the word? Yes: it is a determiner. No: it is a pronoun. A word followed by of is still a pronoun: “Many of the students came.”

Articles a · an · the · no article

An article shows whether the noun is known to the listener or not. a / an is the indefinite article. the is the definite article. Sometimes no article is needed.

NounNot known, or one of manyKnown, or the only oneIn general
Singular countablea / an dogthe doga dog is a pet, or dogs are pets
Plural countable(some) dogsthe dogsdogs (no article)
Uncountable(some) waterthe waterwater (no article)
  • a / an goes only with a singular countable noun. It means one.
  • the goes with any noun. It means both of us know which one.
  • With a plural or uncountable noun in general, use no article.

a or an

a book, a chair, a unit
an apple, an egg, an hour

Choose by the first sound, not the first letter. A consonant sound takes a. A vowel sound takes an. a university starts with a y sound. an hour starts with a vowel sound because the h is silent.

a / an: a new thing

I saw a dog in the park.

The first time you mention something, the listener does not know which one yet.

a / an: what someone is

She is a doctor. He is an engineer.

Use it for jobs, types and identities after be.

a / an: every, per

twice a week, five dollars a kilo, sixty miles an hour

It can mean each or per with time, price and speed.

a / an: all of a kind

A dog is loyal. = Dogs are loyal.

A singular countable noun can speak for the whole group. The plural without an article means the same.

the: both of you know which

Close the door.
I saw a dog. The dog was big.

Use it when the thing is clear from the room, or was mentioned before.

the: only one exists

the sun, the moon, the sky, the world, the internet

the: made clear by what follows

The book on the table is mine.
The girl who called is my cousin.

A phrase or clause after the noun tells which one, so the is used.

the: best, first, same, only, next

the best idea, the first day, the same answer, the only chance, the next bus

Superlatives, ordinals and words such as same, only, next, last point to one thing, so they take the.

the: a whole group

the rich, the elderly, the French
He plays the piano.

Use it before an adjective for a group of people, with nationalities, and with musical instruments.

the: some place names

the Nile, the Pacific, the Alps, the Sahara
the Philippines, the United States, the UK

Rivers, oceans, mountain ranges, deserts, island groups and countries with a plural or union name take the.

No article: in general

Dogs bark. Water is wet. I like music.

A plural or uncountable noun used in a general sense needs no article.

No article: meals, languages, sports, subjects

We have breakfast at eight. She speaks English.
They play tennis. He studies maths.

No article: names

I live in Paris. This is Maria.
Lake Victoria, Mount Everest, India, Oxford Street

Most names of people, cities, countries, lakes, single mountains and streets take no article.

No article: school, work, bed, home

She goes to school by bus. He is at work. I go to bed at ten.
I went to the school to meet a teacher.

For the usual purpose of a place, use no article. For the building itself, use the. Transport after by takes no article: by car, by train, on foot.

No article: times and seasons

at night, at noon, in summer, on Monday
in the morning, in the evening, in the 1990s

Demonstratives this · that · these · those

They point to a noun and show whether it is near or far, one or more than one.

NearFar
One (singular)this bookthat book
More than one (plural)these booksthose books

Near or far in space

This pen in my hand is new.
That pen on the shelf is old.

this / these are close to the speaker. that / those are farther away.

Match the number

this book, these books
that girl, those girls

Wrong: “this books” and “those girl.” Uncountable nouns take the singular: this water, that music.

Time

this morning, this week, these days
that night, that year, those days

this / these are for now and the near future. that / those are for a time in the past, or far away.

Something just said

“We are moving to Spain.” “That idea is wonderful.”

that points back to something already said. this often points forward: “Listen to this story.”

Feelings about a thing

I love this song!
I can’t stand that noise.

Speakers often use this for what they like or feel close to, and that for what they dislike or hold at a distance.

Before a noun, or alone

This cake is good. (determiner)
This is good. (pronoun)
I like this one, not that one.

With no noun, the word is a pronoun. one can replace a singular noun.

That of, those of

The climate of Spain is warmer than that of England.

Formal writing uses that of and those of to avoid repeating a noun.

Possessive determiners my, your, his, her, its, our, their · Sara’s

They show who owns the noun. They are the same words as the possessive adjectives, and they always have a noun after them.

OwnerDeterminerExample
Imymy pen

hehishis car

a nounnoun + ’sSara’s book

Match the owner

Sara loves her brother.
Tom loves his sister.

Choose his or her by the owner, not by the thing owned.

No change for plural nouns

my book, my books

Only the owner changes the word.

Names and nouns with ’s

Sara’s bag, the dog’s tail
the boys’ room, James’s car

Add ’s to a singular noun. For a plural noun ending in s, add only the apostrophe. Names ending in s take either James’s or James’.

Body parts and clothes

He washed his hands. She put on her coat.

English uses a possessive here, not the.

own

I have my own room.
She makes her own bread.

own comes after the possessive and stresses ownership. Wrong: “an own room.”

Only one determiner

my friend (not “the my friend” or “a my friend”)
a friend of mine

To say a with an owner, use of and a possessive pronoun. The same works for this friend of mine.

each other’s

They borrow each other’s books.

Look-alike words

The dog wagged its tail. It’s raining.
their house, over there, they’re late
your bag, you’re late

The possessive has no apostrophe. The apostrophe form is a short verb: it’s = it is, they’re = they are, you’re = you are.

whose

Whose bag is this?
The girl whose bag was lost is here.

whose asks who owns the noun, and it also joins a clause to a noun.

They tell how many or how much. First check whether the noun can be counted.

some or any

I have some ideas.
I don’t have any ideas.
Do you have any ideas?

Positive: some. Negative or a plain question: any. An offer or request also takes some: “Would you like some tea?” In a positive sentence, any means it does not matter which: “Take any seat.”

no, none and not any

I have no money. = I do not have any money.
“How much money?” “None.”

Use only one negative in a clause. With no noun after it, no becomes none (a pronoun).

many and much

How many people came?
I don’t have much time.
She has a lot of friends and a lot of energy.

many goes with plural nouns and much with uncountable ones. In everyday positives, a lot of sounds more natural. After too, so, as and how, many and much are normal: too much sugar, so many people.

few and a few

Few people came. (almost nobody)
A few people came. (some, enough)

few is a negative idea. a few is a positive idea. Both go with plural nouns.

little and a little

There is little time. (almost none)
There is a little time. (some, enough)

The same idea as few and a few, for uncountable nouns.

fewer and less

fewer cars, fewer students
less traffic, less money

fewer for what you can count. less for what you cannot. Time, money and distance in amounts often take less: less than five dollars.

several and enough

She read several books.
We have enough chairs and enough space.

several is plural only. enough comes before the noun.

all, most and half

All children need sleep. (in general)
All the children in my class like football. (a known group)
Most people agree.
Half the cake is gone.

For a general idea, use no the. For a known group, add the, my or these after the quantifier.

Quantifier + of

some of the boys, many of my friends, most of us
(not “some of boys”)

When a determiner or pronoun follows, add of. With a bare noun, leave of out: some boys. all, both and half may skip of: all the boys, both my brothers. These words then stand as pronouns.

Amount words with of

a number of students, a large number of cars
a great deal of money, a large amount of water

Use a number of with plural nouns and an amount of / a great deal of with uncountable nouns.

Numbers one, two, three… · first, second, third…

Cardinal numbers count. Ordinal numbers give the place in a list.

Cardinal numbers

one book, two books, ten books

one goes with a singular noun. Every other number goes with a plural noun. Wrong: “two book.”

Ordinal numbers

the first day, the second chapter, the third time

Ordinals usually take the. Without it, they mean one more: a second chance.

Order of first, last and numbers

the first two days
the last three chapters
(not “the two first days”)

The ordinal comes before the cardinal number.

Number + noun as an adjective

a ten-year-old boy, a two-day trip
The boy is ten years old.

In a compound before a noun, the unit stays singular and takes hyphens.

Another with a number

I need another two weeks.

another usually goes with a singular noun, but it can come before a number and a plural noun.

Each, every, either, neither, both distributives

They talk about the members of a group one by one, or two together.

DeterminerGroup sizeNoun after itExample
eachTwo or moreSingularEach child has a pen.
everyThree or moreSingularEvery student passed.
eitherExactly twoSingularTake either road.
neitherExactly twoSingularNeither answer is right.
bothExactly twoPluralBoth books are mine.

each and every

Each student gets a different book. (one at a time)
Every student passed. (all, as a group)

Both take a singular noun and a singular verb. each looks at individuals. every looks at the whole group.

every for time and repeats

every day, every two weeks, every other day
Almost every child likes sweets.

every works with almost, nearly, practically and with periods of time. each does not.

each and every with of

each of the boys has a pen. (a pronoun)
every one of the boys has a pen.

every cannot stand alone before of. Use every one of or each of. The verb stays singular.

either

You can sit on either side.
I don’t like either answer.

It means one or the other, of two. It can also mean both: on either side of the road.

neither

Neither answer is correct.

It means not one and not the other. Do not add not: “Neither answer is not right” is wrong.

both

Both books are mine.
Both my brothers play chess.
Both of the books are mine.

both takes a plural noun and a plural verb. It can come before the, my, these.

another, other, the other

another book (one more)
other books (different ones)
the other book, the other books (the rest)

another is an + other, so it goes with singular nouns. other goes with plural and uncountable nouns. the other goes with either, when the group is known.

Question and relative determiners which, what, whose

They stand before a noun to ask for it or to join a clause to it.

which: a choice from a known set

Which bag is yours?
Which books did you buy?

Use it when the choices are limited and known.

what: an open choice

What time is it?
What music do you like?

Use it when there are many possible answers or the set is not known.

whose: the owner

Whose pen is this?

Relative whose

The girl whose bag was lost is here.

whose joins a clause to a noun and shows ownership. It works for people, animals and things.

whichever and whatever

Take whichever seat you like.
Whatever time you come, I will be home.

They mean it does not matter which one.

what in exclamations

What a day!
What beautiful flowers!
What nonsense!

Use what a / an with a singular countable noun. Use what alone with plural and uncountable nouns.

Words that come before the article all, both, half, such, what, quite, double, twice

A few words can stand before a, the, this, my. They are called predeterminers.

WordPatternExample
all, both, half+ the / my / these + nounall the children, both my brothers, half the price
double, twice, three times+ the + nountwice the cost
such+ a / an + (adjective) + nounsuch a day, such nice people

quite, rather+ a / an + nounquite a long way, rather a surprise
half+ a / an + nounhalf an hour, half a day

such and so

It was such a nice day.
The day was so nice.

such goes before a noun phrase. so goes before an adjective or adverb alone. Wrong: “so a nice day.”

Other words that act like determiners

certain people, various reasons, different ideas
the same answer, the whole day

These words point to the noun without being an adjective of quality. They follow the main determiner.

One determiner only

“the my book” → my book
“a this house” → this house

Articles, demonstratives and possessives cannot be stacked. The words in the first column of the order table are the exception.

The noun is left out

I bought two books. I bought two.
This bag is red. This is red.

When the noun is understood, the determiner stands alone and becomes a pronoun. my becomes mine, and no becomes none.

Determiners with other nouns

the Smiths, the United States
Maria, Mr Smith, tomorrow (no determiner)

Names usually take none. A family name in the plural takes the.

Singular words with a plural meaning

Every student has a book.
Each child was given a prize.

The determiner is singular, so the verb is singular too, even when many people are meant.

How to choose the right determiner

Ask these questions in order. Each answer narrows the list.

1. Is the noun countable?

  1. Countable and singular: a / an, the, this, that, my, each, every, either, neither, another, one.
  2. Countable and plural: the, these, those, my, some, any, many, few, several, both, two, or no determiner.
  3. Uncountable: the, this, that, my, some, any, much, little, less, enough, or no determiner.

2. Does the listener know which one?

  1. Yes, or only one exists: the.
  2. No, and it is one of many: a / an (singular), some (plural or uncountable).
  3. You mean the whole group in general: no article.

3. Does someone own it?

  1. Use my, your, his, her, its, our, their or a noun with ’s.
  2. Do not add a or the.

4. Is it near or far?

  1. Near: this (one), these (more than one).
  2. Far: that (one), those (more than one).

5. Do you need an amount?

  1. Exact number: one, two, three…
  2. Large: many (count), much (cannot count), a lot of (both).
  3. Small: few / a few (count), little / a little (cannot count).
  4. Group members: each, every, either, neither, both.

Practice

Answers are hidden. Try each question first, then tap Answer.

Part A A, an, the or no article

  1. She has umbrella. (a / an / the)
    Answer

    an. umbrella starts with a vowel sound.

  2. He studies at university. (a / an)
    Answer

    a. university starts with a y sound.

  3. It takes hour. (a / an)
    Answer

    an. The h is silent.

  4. I saw a cat. cat was black. (A / The)
    Answer

    The. The cat was mentioned before.

  5. My mother is nurse. (a / the / no article)
    Answer

    a. A job after be.

  6. sun rises in the east. (A / The)
    Answer

    The. There is only one sun.

  7. She is tallest girl in class. (a / the)
    Answer

    the. A superlative.

  8. We play tennis on Sundays. (the / no article)
    Answer

    no article. Sports take none.

  9. He plays guitar well. (the / no article)
    Answer

    the. A musical instrument.

  10. dogs are loyal. (The / no article) General meaning.
    Answer

    no article. Plural noun in general.

  11. They sailed across Pacific. (the / no article)
    Answer

    the. Names of oceans take the.

  12. Children go to school to learn. (the / no article)
    Answer

    no article. The usual purpose of a school.

Part B This, that and possessives

  1. The pen in my hand: pen. (this / that)
    Answer

    this. It is near.

  2. The books on the far shelf: books. (these / those)
    Answer

    those. Far and plural.

  3. Tom loves sister. (his / her)
    Answer

    his. Match the owner, Tom.

  4. Sara loves brother. (his / her)
    Answer

    her. The owner is Sara.

  5. The dog wagged tail. (its / it’s)
    Answer

    its. No apostrophe for ownership.

  6. Fix: a my friend
    Answer

    a friend of mine (or my friend). One determiner only.

  7. bag is this? (Who’s / Whose)
    Answer

    Whose. It asks about the owner of the noun.

  8. That is the dog’s bowl. Which word is the determiner?
    Answer

    the dog’s. It tells whose bowl. That is the pronoun here.

Part C Some, any, many, much, few, little

  1. I would like water. (some / any)
    Answer

    some. A request.

  2. I don’t have time. (some / any)
    Answer

    any. A negative statement.

  3. How people came? (many / much)
    Answer

    many. People can be counted.

  4. How sugar do you take? (many / much)
    Answer

    much. sugar is uncountable.

  5. He has friends. Almost none. (few / a few)
    Answer

    few. It means almost none.

  6. We have time. Enough to finish. (little / a little)
    Answer

    a little. It means some.

  7. There are cars today. (fewer / less)
    Answer

    fewer. Cars can be counted.

  8. She read books on the trip. (several / much)
    Answer

    several. much does not go with plural nouns.

  9. of the boys were late. (Some / Much)
    Answer

    Some. boys are countable, and much goes only with uncountable nouns.

  10. I have homework. Not any. (no / none)
    Answer

    no. A noun follows, so no. Alone it would be none.

Part D Each, every, both, either, another

  1. student has a locker. (Each / Both)
    Answer

    Each. both needs a plural noun.

  2. Every child a book. (has / have)
    Answer

    has. every is singular.

  3. books are mine. (Both / Either)
    Answer

    Both. A plural noun, two books.

  4. You can take road. There are two. (either / every)
    Answer

    either. One or the other of two.

  5. answer is correct. Not one of the two. (Neither / Either)
    Answer

    Neither. Not one and not the other.

  6. Would you like cup of tea? (another / other)
    Answer

    another. One more, singular.

  7. Some people like tea. people like coffee. (Another / Other)
    Answer

    Other. Different people, plural.

  8. One shoe is here. Where is shoe? (another / the other)
    Answer

    the other. The one remaining of a known pair.

  9. Put these in order: (a) days (b) two (c) the (d) first
    Answer

    the first two days. Ordinal before cardinal.

  10. Put these in order: (a) friends (b) all (c) old (d) my
    Answer

    all my old friends. Before the article, the determiner, then the adjective, then the noun.

  11. It was nice day. (such / so)
    Answer

    such. such a nice day. so would go with an adjective alone.

  12. a surprise! (What / Which)
    Answer

    What. An exclamation uses what.
