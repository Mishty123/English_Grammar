English Grammar Reference

Minor Fixed Structures

Fifteen smaller systems that sit alongside the five Fundamental Dimensions. Each has its own fixed formula — a slot-and-filler pattern that doesn't bend with context.

On this page

    Comparison

    1 · Comparison

    Degrees of Comparison

    The three-way scale every gradable adjective/adverb follows.

    FormFormula
    Positiveas + adjective/adverb + as
    Comparativeadjective-er / more + adjective + than
    Superlativethe + adjective-est / the most + adjective
    • Mario is as brave as his friends.
    • The chair was more mysterious than any prize.
    • Guneet was the most loyal of all.
    Rule to know: One-syllable and most two-syllable adjectives take −er/−est; longer adjectives switch to more/most. Never combine both forms.

    2 · Comparison

    Comparative Correlative

    Two comparatives locked together to show one quantity changing in step with another.

    FormFormula
    FormulaThe + comparative …, the + comparative …
    • The longer he tried, the more his classmates laughed.
    Rule to know: Both halves front “the + comparative” before their own subject and verb — no “than” appears anywhere in the pattern.

    Result & Degree

    3 · Result & Degree

    So…that / Such…that

    Links a degree of quality to a resulting consequence.

    FormFormula
    soso + adjective/adverb + that + clause
    suchsuch (+ a/an) + adjective + noun + that + clause
    • The chair was so strange that nobody believed him.
    • It was such a strange chair that nobody believed him.
    Rule to know: Use “so” directly before an adjective/adverb with no noun; switch to “such” the moment a noun is involved.

    4 · Result & Degree

    Too…to / Enough…to

    Expresses excess (too) or sufficiency (enough) relative to an implied outcome.

    FormFormula
    tootoo + adjective/adverb + to + verb
    enoughadjective/adverb + enough + to + verb
    • The chair was too strange to see clearly.
    • Mario was brave enough to try again.
    Rule to know: “Enough” always follows the word it modifies — the opposite order from most modifiers. “Too” always comes before it.

    Purpose & Concession

    5 · Purpose & Concession

    Purpose Clauses

    States the intention behind an action, with or without its own subject.

    FormFormula
    with subjectso that + subject + can/could/will/would + verb
    no subjectin order to / so as to + base verb
    • He held the chair so that Mario wouldn't fall.
    • He asked for help in order to test his friends.
    Rule to know: “So that” needs a full clause with its own subject and modal; “to / in order to / so as to” attach straight to a bare infinitive.

    6 · Purpose & Concession

    Concessive Clauses

    Acknowledges a contrasting fact that doesn't stop the main outcome.

    FormFormula
    clausealthough / even though / though + clause
    phrasedespite / in spite of + noun phrase / gerund
    • Although he fell twice, Mario kept trying.
    • Despite falling twice, Mario kept trying.
    Rule to know: “Despite” and “in spite of” can never be followed by a full clause with its own subject and verb — only a noun phrase or gerund.

    Verb Patterns

    7 · Verb Patterns

    Causative Constructions

    Shows the subject arranging for something to happen rather than doing it directly.

    FormFormula
    done to objecthave/get + object + past participle
    person actshave + person + base verb / get + person + to + verb
    • Mario had the chair delivered.
    • Grandpa got Mario to test his friends.
    Rule to know: “Have someone do something” takes the bare infinitive; “get someone to do something” always needs “to.”

    8 · Verb Patterns

    Preference Structures

    States a preference between actions, with a special tense rule when the subject changes.

    FormFormula
    same subjectwould rather + base verb
    different subjectwould rather + subject + past simple
    generalprefer + gerund + to + gerund
    • Mario would rather test his friends than assume.
    • Mario would rather his grandpa didn't interfere.
    • He prefers testing friendships to guessing.
    Rule to know: When the subject changes after “would rather,” the following verb drops to simple past even though the meaning is present/future.

    9 · Verb Patterns

    Gerund vs. Infinitive Patterns

    Which non-finite form follows a verb is fixed per verb, not by a general rule.

    FormFormula
    gerundverb + -ing (avoid, enjoy, suggest, mind …)
    infinitiveverb + to + verb (decide, want, agree, hope …)
    eitherverb + either, meaning changes (stop, remember, try …)
    • He avoided sitting incorrectly again.
    • He decided to try again.
    • He stopped falling. vs. He stopped to check the chair.
    Rule to know: There's no shortcut rule — the choice is lexical (memorized per verb), except for the small set of verbs where both forms exist and change meaning.

    Questions & Emphasis

    10 · Questions & Emphasis

    Embedded / Indirect Questions

    A question folded inside a larger, more polite sentence.

    FormFormula
    Formulareporting frame + if/whether or wh-word + subject + verb (no inversion)
    • Could you tell me where the chair is?
    • I wonder if he'll try again.
    Rule to know: Word order returns to normal statement order even though it still ends in a question; “if/whether” stands in for yes/no question words.

    11 · Questions & Emphasis

    Question Tags

    A mini yes/no question appended to confirm a statement.

    FormFormula
    positive statement+ negative tag
    negative statement+ positive tag (matching auxiliary + subject pronoun)
    • Mario fell again, didn't he?
    • He wasn't ready, was he?
    Rule to know: The tag's auxiliary must match the main verb's tense exactly, and the tag's subject is always a pronoun — never the full noun repeated.

    12 · Questions & Emphasis

    Cleft / Emphatic Sentences

    Reorders a sentence to spotlight one specific part of it.

    FormFormula
    it-cleftIt + is/was + [focus] + that/who + rest of clause
    wh-cleftWhat + clause + is/was + focus
    • It was Mario who won the bet.
    • What surprised them was his balance.
    Rule to know: The it-cleft uses “that” for things and may use “who” for people; the wh-cleft always places the focused element last.

    Other Fixed Frames

    13 · Other Fixed Frames

    Correlative Conjunctions

    Paired linking words that join two balanced elements.

    FormFormula
    Pairsnot only … but also / either … or / neither … nor / both … and
    • Not only did he fall, but he also refused to give up.
    • Neither the chair nor the bet scared him.
    Rule to know: In “either/or” and “neither/nor,” the verb agrees with the noun closest to it — “Neither the boys nor Mario was afraid.”

    14 · Other Fixed Frames

    Existential “There”

    Introduces the existence of something without “there” acting as the real subject.

    FormFormula
    FormulaThere + be + noun phrase (+ location/time)
    • There were three loyal friends among the crowd.
    • There was only one seat left when he arrived.
    Rule to know: “Be” agrees with the noun phrase that follows it, not with “there” itself — a common subject-verb agreement trap.

    15 · Other Fixed Frames

    Wish / If Only

    Expresses a present wish, a past regret, or a complaint about someone else's behavior.

    FormFormula
    present wishwish/if only + past simple
    past regretwish/if only + past perfect
    complaintwish/if only + would + base verb
    • I wish I had real friends like that.
    • If only he had tested them sooner.
    • I wish they would stop laughing.
    Rule to know: “Wish” pushes the verb one tense further into the past than its real-world time reference — present wishes use simple past, past regrets use past perfect.
