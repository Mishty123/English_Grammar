What a noun shows

Five things can change or be checked about any noun. Each one is covered in the steps that follow.

Kind

Common, proper, abstract, collective, material or compound. The kind decides capital letters and the verb.

Countable or not

Can you count it? This decides a / an, many / much and fewer / less.

Number

One (singular) or more than one (plural). The spelling of the plural has rules.

Gender

Masculine, feminine, common or neuter. English only shows it in the word itself and the pronoun.

Case

The job of the noun. Only the possessive case changes the form (boy’s).

The verb rule for every noun

Every noun is 3rd person. Use it to pick the verb.

The noun isIt works likeVerb formExample
Singular (one)he / she / itV5 (plays, has, is)The boy plays.
Plural (more than one)theyV1 (play, have, are)The boys play.
UncountableitV5The water is cold.

Some nouns bend this rule (news, police, a team, a pair of scissors). They are all in the Agreement tab.

Common and proper nouns

Two kinds of name: one for the whole group, and one for the exact thing.

In plain words

A common noun is the folder name (river, month, language). It labels a whole group of things of the same kind.

A proper noun is the exact file name inside that folder (the Nile, June, Telugu). It points to the one you mean, so it gets a capital letter.

What takes a capital letter

Names of people, places, days, months, holidays, languages, nationalities, religions, brands, books, films and titles used with a name.

She speaks French and Hindi.
We meet on Friday in June.
Professor Lee teaches at the university.

The four seasons are common nouns and take no capital: spring, summer, autumn, winter. Compass points are common too (go north) unless they name a region (the South).

A proper noun used as a common noun

When a name stands for a class, a type or more than one person, it behaves like a common noun.

He is the Newton of our school. (a great scientist)
There are three Marys in my class.
The Joneses live next door. (the Jones family)

A common noun used as a proper noun

I asked Mother for help. (used as her name)
I asked my mother for help.
The President arrived. / He is the president of the club.

A family word or a title used as a name takes a capital. After my, the or a it is common again.

A concrete noun can be sensed. An abstract noun names something you cannot see or touch.

Concrete nouns

Concrete nouns can be common (table), proper (Delhi), collective (flock) or material (gold).

How to find an abstract noun

Ask: can I see it, touch it or hear it? If not, it is abstract. Many are built from adjectives, verbs or nouns (see the Word building tab).

She was famous for her bravery. (from brave)
His failure surprised us. (from fail)
Their friendship lasted for years. (from friend)

Material nouns

A material noun names what a thing is made of. Most are uncountable.

Common material nouns

goldsilverironcopperwoodglasscottonsilkwoolleatherplasticpaperwatermilkoilsugarricecementsandsteel

No a / an, no plural

Gold is expensive.
This ring is made of gold.
Wrong: a gold  ·  golds

To count a material, use a unit: a piece of wood, a bar of gold, a glass of milk, a bag of rice.

Made of, made from, made out of

The table is made of wood. (you can still see the wood)
Paper is made from wood. (the wood has changed into something new)
The toy is made out of old boxes. (reused material)

A material noun can become a common noun

Windows are made of glass. / She drank a glass of water. / He wears glasses.
The shed is made of iron. / She pressed her shirt with an iron.

The meaning changes when the noun becomes countable. More of these are in the Countable tab.

Countable and Uncountable Nouns

Nouns can be divided into two types based on countability: countable and uncountable.

  • Countable nouns refer to things you can count individually. Because they have number, they have both singular and plural forms (one apple, two apples). Plural rules: Number & Gender tab →
  • Uncountable nouns refer to things you cannot count individually, such as liquids, substances, or abstract ideas (water, information).

How Determiners Fit In

Depending on whether a noun is countable or uncountable, specific determiners come before it to show which one or how many (for example, a or many for countable nouns vs. much for uncountable nouns).

🔤

Articles

👉

Demonstratives

🎒

Possessives

my, your, his, her, its, our, their

🔢

Numbers

⚖️

Quantifiers

many, much, few, a few, little, a little, some, any, several, enough, no, all, most, more, less, fewer, a lot of

Rule: the determiner must match the noun. a goes with a singular countable noun, many with a plural countable noun, much with an uncountable noun.

Use them in different ways

Exams often ask you to use one noun in two ways. Here is the pattern.

One noun, two meanings

One noun, a unit word

Her advice was good.
She gave me two pieces of advice.

Choose the quantity word

How many eggs do we need?
How much flour do we need?
He has few friends, so he feels lonely.

The four genders

English nouns do not change for gender the way many languages do. Gender shows in the meaning of the word and in the pronoun that replaces it.

The cases of a noun

The case of a noun is its job in the sentence. Books name the cases differently, so know both sets of names.

CaseThe noun isExampleForm
Nominativethe subject, or the complement after a linking verbThe boy sleeps. Tom is a doctor.boy
Objectivethe object of a verb or a prepositionShe called the boy. He sat by the boy.boy
Possessiveshowing who or what owns somethingthe boy’s bagboy’s
Vocativethe person spoken to (direct address)Tom, come here.Tom

Newer books say English nouns have only two cases: the common case (no change) and the genitive case (the possessive). The nominative and objective look the same, so the job is found from the sentence.

The objective case

A noun is in the objective case when it receives the action or follows a preposition.

Direct object

She fed the dog.

Ask: She fed what?

Indirect object

She gave the dog a bone.

Ask: To whom or for whom?

Object of a preposition

He sat on the chair.

Adverbial object

We waited an hour.
She walked a mile.

A time, distance or measure noun with no preposition.

Retained and cognate objects

He was given a prize. (active: They gave him a prize.)

She sang a song.  ·  He lived a happy life.  ·  They fought a good fight.

Use it in different ways

Case questions ask you to change the form, name the case, or rewrite a phrase.

Rewrite with of or ’s

the book of my friend → my friend’s book

Name the case

The jobs a noun can do

The same noun can do different jobs in different sentences. Find the job by asking a question about the verb.

Coloursthe nounthe verbsmall word

Subject

Who or what does the action, or is described?

The dog barked.
Honesty pays.

Direct object

Who or what receives the action?

She fed the dog.
He wrote a letter.

Indirect object

To whom or for whom was it done?

She gave the dog a bone.
He wrote his mother a letter.

Object of a preposition

The noun that follows a preposition.

He sat on the chair.
She is afraid of spiders.

Subject complement

A noun after a linking verb (be, become, seem, remain) that names or describes the subject. It means the same person or thing as the subject.

My brother is a doctor.
She became captain of the team.
Tom remained my best friend.

The verb agrees with the subject, not the complement. “The cause of the riot was high prices.”

Object complement

A noun after the object that names or renames it. Verbs: call, name, make, elect, appoint, consider, choose.

They elected him president.
We call the baby Sam.
The film made her a star.

Apposition

A noun placed beside another noun to explain it. Both name the same person or thing. Commas usually separate an apposition that adds extra information.

My friend Tom, a doctor, lives here.
Delhi, the capital of India, is very large.
We visited the poet Tagore’s house.

Test: take one noun out. The sentence should still work.

Direct address (vocative)

The name or title of the person you speak to. It is cut off by commas.

Anna, come here.
Thank you, sir.
Yes, doctor, I will.

Adverbial

A noun of time, place, distance or manner used like an adverb, with no preposition.

He left Monday.
We waited an hour.
She walked a mile.
They live next door.  ·  Come this way.

Noun adjunct (noun used as a modifier)

A noun placed before another noun to describe it. It acts like an adjective but stays a noun.

a school bus  ·  a stone wall  ·  a shoe shop  ·  a tennis match

The modifier is usually singular: a shoe shop, not “a shoes shop.” Exceptions use a plural form: a clothes shop, a savings account, a customs officer, an arms race, a sales manager.

Nominative absolute

A noun with a participle, standing apart from the rest of the sentence.

The work done, we went home.
The sun having set, the birds fell silent.

Exclamation

What a day!  ·  Fire!  ·  Such kindness!

Words that do a noun’s job

A noun is not the only word that can be a subject or an object. These are called noun equivalents.

Gerund (verb + -ing as a noun)

Swimming is good exercise.
She enjoys reading.
He is good at drawing.

A gerund can take an object: She enjoys reading novels. With the + of it is a verbal noun: the reading of the will. Compare: Reading the will took an hour.

Infinitive (to + verb)

To err is human.
I want to rest.

Pronoun

She called me.
Everyone agreed.

Noun phrase

The tall man in the grey coat waved.
I like cold orange juice.

A noun phrase is a noun with its determiner, adjectives and other modifiers. The noun is the head word, and the verb agrees with it.

Noun clause

What he said is true.
I know that she is honest.
Tell me where you live.

A noun clause has its own subject and verb. It is covered with the clauses.

An adjective with the

The rich are not always happy.
The young need guidance.
The poor deserve help.

These mean groups of people and take a plural verb. They have no -s.

Numbers and words used as words

Seven is a lucky number.
“And” is a conjunction.
There are no ifs or buts.

The basic rule

The verb agrees with the head noun of the subject. V5 is the singular form (plays, has, is). V1 is the plural form (play, have, are).

A singular noun takes V5, like he, she, it. A plural noun takes V1, like they. An uncountable noun takes V5.

Ignore the words between

A phrase between the subject and the verb does not change the verb.

The box of chocolates is empty.
The price of the apples has risen.
The boys in the front row are talking.

The subject comes after the verb

There is a book on the table.
There are two books on the table.
Here comes the bus.
Where are the keys?

In there is / there are, the verb agrees with the noun that follows. If a list follows, formal writing agrees with the first noun: There is a table and two chairs.

The verb agrees with the subject, not the complement

My favourite subject is languages.
The cause of the fire was faulty wires.

Two nouns joined together

The joining word decides the verb.

Joined byThe verb matchesExamples
andBoth together: plural (V1)The boy and the girl are here.  Tom and Mary have left.
and for one thing or personOne unit: singular (V5)Bread and butter is my breakfast.  The poet and statesman is dead. (one man)
each / every + noun and nounSingular (V5)Every boy and girl has a book.  Each man and woman was paid.
or, nor, either…or, neither…norThe nearer nounNeither the teacher nor the students were late.  Neither the students nor the teacher was late.
with, along with, as well as, together with, in addition toThe first noun onlyThe teacher, along with the students, is here.  The students, along with the teacher, are here.

The pronoun that follows agrees too: Neither the boys nor Mary finished her work. With or / nor, put the plural noun last when you can. It sounds more natural.

Collective nouns

The verb depends on how you think of the group.

One unit: singular

The committee has reached its decision.
The team is winning.
My family is small.

Separate members: plural

The committee are divided in their opinions.
The team are changing their shirts.
My family are all doctors.

Keep verb and pronoun together

The team is doing its best.
The team are doing their best.

The team is doing their best.
The team are doing its best.

American English almost always uses the singular. British English accepts both. If an exam gives its or their after the verb, match it.

Always plural

peoplepolicecattleclergygentrypoultryvermin

The police are here.  The cattle are in the shed.

Amounts, fractions and quantity phrases

Think about the whole or the parts.

Money, time, distance and weight as one amount

Ten rupees is not much.
Two years is a long time.
Five miles is a long way.
Three kilos is enough.

When you count the separate periods or items, use the plural: Ten years have passed since then.

Fractions and percentages

The verb agrees with the noun after of.

Half of the cake is gone.
Two-thirds of the work is done.
Fifty percent of the water is wasted.

Half of the cakes are gone.
Two-thirds of the students are girls.
Fifty percent of the people agree.

a number of / the number of

A number of students are absent.

The number of students is small.

The same difference applies to a variety of / the variety of and a group of / the group of. A makes the following noun the subject, and the makes the first noun the subject.

a lot of, plenty of, most of, some of, all of

A lot of time was wasted.
A lot of people were waiting.
Most of the money is gone.  Most of the books are old.

one of, many a, more than one

One of my friends is a doctor.
Many a student has failed.
More than one student has complained.

The verb is singular because the real subject is one, many a + a singular noun, or more than one + a singular noun.

one of those who

He is one of those students who work hard.

The relative word who stands for the plural noun students, so the verb is plural.

Part E Case

  1. Write the possessive: the bag of the boy; the bags of the boys
    Answer

    the boy’s bag; the boys’ bags.

  2. Write the possessive: the toys of the children; the clothes of the women
    Answer

    the children’s toys; the women’s clothes. Irregular plurals take ’s.

  3. Possessive of: my mother-in-law
    Answer

    my mother-in-law’s. The ending goes on the last word.

  4. One house, owned by Tom and Mary together. Write the possessive.
    Answer

    Tom and Mary’s house. For separate houses: Tom’s and Mary’s houses.

  5. Which is correct? (a) the legs of the table (b) the table’s legs
    Answer

    (a). Use of for things. (b) is common in speech, but exams prefer (a).

  6. Fill in: She went on holiday. (a weeks / a week’s / a weeks’)
    Answer

    a week’s. One week, possessive.

  7. Name the case of each underlined noun: Anna, bring me the teacher’s book.
    Answer

    Anna: vocative. teacher’s: possessive.

  8. What does at the baker’s mean?
    Answer

    At the baker’s shop. The noun is left out after ’s.

  9. Explain: a picture of Tom’s and a picture of Tom.
    Answer

    The first is a picture Tom owns. The second is a picture that shows Tom.

  10. Correct: The girls bag is heavy.
    Answer

    The girls’ bag is heavy.

Part F Jobs of a noun

  1. Job of dog: The dog barked.
    Answer

    Subject.

  2. Job of letter: He wrote a letter.
    Answer

    Direct object. He wrote what?

  3. Job of mother: He wrote his mother a letter.
    Answer

    Indirect object. To whom?

  4. Job of doctor: My brother is a doctor.
    Answer

    Subject complement. It describes the subject.

  5. Job of president: They elected him president.
    Answer

    Object complement. It renames the object.

  6. Job of capital: Delhi, the capital of India, is large.
    Answer

    Apposition. It explains Delhi.

  7. Job of hour: We waited an hour.
    Answer

    Adverbial (a noun of time, used like an adverb).

  8. Job of school: The school bus is late.
    Answer

    Noun adjunct. It modifies bus.

Part G Agreement

  1. The boy and the girl here. (is / are)
    Answer

    are. Joined by and: plural.

  2. Bread and butter my breakfast. (is / are)
    Answer

    is. One unit.

  3. Every boy and girl a book. (has / have)
    Answer

    has. every makes it V5.

  4. Neither the teacher nor the students late. (was / were)
    Answer

    were. The nearer noun, students.

  5. The teacher, along with the students, here. (is / are)
    Answer

    is. The first noun counts.

  6. The committee reached its decision. (has / have)
    Answer

    has. One unit, and its shows it.

  7. The committee divided in their opinions. (is / are)
    Answer

    are. Members acting separately, and their shows it.

  8. The police here. (is / are)
    Answer

    are. police is always plural.

  9. The news good. (is / are)
    Answer

    is. Plural in form, singular in meaning.

  10. My trousers torn. (is / are)
    Answer

    are. A two-part noun is plural.

  11. A pair of scissors on the desk. (is / are)
    Answer

    is. The verb agrees with pair.

  12. A number of students absent. (is / are)
    Answer

    are. a number of is plural.

  13. The number of students small. (is / are)
    Answer

    is. the number is the subject.

  14. Half of the cake gone. (is / are)
    Answer

    is. The verb follows cake.

  15. Half of the cakes gone. (is / are)
    Answer

    are. The verb follows cakes.

  16. Two years a long time. (is / are)
    Answer

    is. One period of time.

  17. One of the boys late. (is / are)
    Answer

    is. The subject is one.

  18. Many a student failed. (has / have)
    Answer

    has. many a + singular noun: V5.

  19. The poor help. (needs / need)
    Answer

    need. the poor means poor people: V1.

  20. The United States a large country. (is / are)
    Answer

    is. A country name is one thing.

Part J Use it in different ways

Write your own sentences, then tap Answer to compare. Your sentences will differ. Check that each one follows the rule.

  1. Use time as an uncountable noun and as a countable noun.
    Answer

    Uncountable: I do not have much time. Countable: I have visited the museum three times.

  2. Use paper in two senses: the material and a newspaper.
    Answer

    Material: I need some paper to write on. Newspaper: My father reads a paper every morning.

  3. Use the collective noun team with a singular verb and with a plural verb.
    Answer

    Singular: The team is winning the match. Plural: The team are arguing about their positions.
