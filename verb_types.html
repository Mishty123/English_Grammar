Verb types

Who does what

The lexical verb, the helping verbs and the non-finite forms: four tabs: the lexical verb, the helping verbs, and the finite and non-finite forms.

The lexical verb carries the meaning. It is the last role in the verb phrase.

1

Categorizing lexical verbs by what follows them

This is the sorting that sets the clause pattern. The lexical verb is the last role in the verb phrase.

TypeWhat follows the verbPatternExample
IntransitivenothingNone (S V)The baby slept.
Transitivean objectO (S V O)She kicked the ball.
Ambitransitivean object, or nothing: the verb works both waysO or noneHe ate. (no object)
He ate an apple. (object)
Ditransitivetwo objects: who gets it, then whatO + OHe gave her a book.
Linking (copular)a complement that describes or renames the subjectC (S V C)The soup is hot.

Test for an object

After the verb ask what? or whom? If there is an answer, the verb is transitive.

She kicked the ball. (kicked what?)

Linking verbs

A linking verb connects the subject to a description. The commonest are be, seem, become, look, feel, sound, taste, smell, appear, remain, get and turn.

She seems tired. The cake smells good.

Complement and object are not the same thing. A complement describes or renames the subject (after a linking verb). An object receives the action (after a transitive verb).

Watch out The same verb can change type with its meaning. She looks tired. (linking) She felt the sound. (transitive) Look at what follows the verb, not at the verb alone.

3

By structure: phrasal and causative

Some lexical verbs are built from more than one word, or make someone else act.

Phrasal verbs (verb + particle)

A phrasal verb is a verb with a small word such as up, down, off, out, after. Together they have one meaning, often different from the verb alone.

KindWhat it doesExample
Separablethe object can go in the middle or after the particleShe picked up the phone. / She picked it up.
Inseparablethe object always comes after the particleShe looks after the baby. (not looked the baby after)
Intransitiveno object at allThe car broke down.

Causative verbs

A causative verb says that someone makes or lets another person do something. The four to know are make, have, let, get.

VerbPatternExample
makemake + object + bare infinitiveShe made him laugh.
havehave + object + bare infinitiveI had the barber cut my hair.
letlet + object + bare infinitiveHe let me go.
getget + object + to-infinitiveI got him to wash the car.

Causative verbs sit in the Verb slot. What follows them is a non-finite verb. See the Non-finite tab for the bare infinitive.

4

By form: regular or irregular

The five forms of a verb are V1 to V5. A regular verb adds -ed. An irregular verb changes in its own way.

FormNameRegular: walkIrregular: write
V1base formwalkwrite
V2simple pastwalkedwrote
V3past participlewalkedwritten
V4-ing formwalkingwriting
V53rd person singular, present (-s / -es)walkswrites

Regular verbs have the same V2 and V3 (walked, walked). Irregular verbs often differ (write, wrote, written), and some do not change at all (put, put, put).

Helping (auxiliary) verbs come before the lexical verb and set its form.

1

Three kinds of helping verbs

A helping verb stands before the lexical verb. It adds little meaning of its own. It sets the tense, the aspect or the voice, or it makes a question or a negative.

Primary

be, have, do

The three verbs that can be a helper or a lexical verb.

She is eating. She is tired.

Modal

can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must

Helpers only. They add a meaning such as ability, possibility or duty.

She can swim.

Semi-modal

ought to, have to, used to, be going to, be about to, be able to

Two or three words that work like a modal.

She has to leave early.

KindCan also be a lexical verb?Changes with the subject (-s)?
Primary: be, have, doyesyes: is, has, does
Modalno, neverno: she can, not she cans
Semi-modalsome can: have to, be able tothe first word does: she has to

In a verb phrase the helper is the first verb, so it is finite (Role 1). It gives the tense. The lexical verb at the end is the head of the phrase.

2

Be, have, do and the modals

Be, have and do can be a helper or a lexical verb. Modals are always helpers.

Be, have and do: helper or lexical verb

The same word can do either job. Look at what follows it.

Modals

MeaningModalsExample
Abilitycan, couldShe can swim.
Possibilitymay, might, couldIt might rain tonight.
Permissioncan, mayYou may go now.
Duty and advicemust, shouldYou must wear a helmet.
Future and predictionwill, shall, wouldHe will travel tomorrow.

Four rules for modals

  • No -s: she can, not she cans.
  • No to: the next verb is a bare infinitive. He can drive. not He can to drive.
  • No -ing or V3 form of their own. A modal is always Role 1.
  • Questions and negatives need no do: Can she swim? She can not swim.

Semi-modals

Semi-modalMeaningExample

Also used with a bare infinitive: had better, would rather, would sooner. You had better hurry.

A finite verb has a tense and agrees with its subject. It is always the first verb in the verb phrase.

1

What makes a verb finite

A finite verb is a verb form with a tense and a subject. Each clause has one finite verb, and it is the first verb of the verb phrase.

A finite verb changes with tense, person and number and can be the only verb of a sentence. A non-finite verb cannot.

FeatureFinite verbNon-finite verb
Shows tenseYes (walks, walked)No
Agrees with subjectYes (he walks, they walk)No
Can be the only verb of a sentenceYesNo
ExampleShe wants to leave.She wants to leave.

Which verb in the phrase is finite?

Always the first one (Role 1). Every verb after it is non-finite.

Verb phraseFinite (Role 1)Non-finite (later roles)
She sings.sings—
She is singing.issinging
She has been singing.hasbeen, singing
She might have been singing.mighthave, been, singing

Why it matters: only the finite verb takes the tense and agrees with the subject. She has been singing (has, not have) and She was singing. The later verbs never change for the subject.

2

Finite verbs by mood

Mood is the attitude a finite verb shows: a fact, a command or a wish.

MoodUseExample
Indicativefacts, statements, questionsShe sings every day. Does she sing?
Imperativecommands, requests, adviceClose the door. Be quiet.
Subjunctivewishes, unreal situations, formal demandsIf I were you, I would wait. I suggest that he leave early.

The imperative uses the bare verb (V1) and has no stated subject. The subjunctive often looks like a plain V1 (he leave, with no -s) or uses were for every person.

3

Lexical or helping: finite verbs are both

Finite is not a third kind of verb. A lexical verb or a helping verb is finite when it stands first in the verb phrase.

Finite verbKindExample
singslexical verb aloneShe sings.
ishelping verbShe is singing.
canmodal helping verbShe can sing.
islexical (linking) verbShe is tired.
haslexical verb: ownsShe has two bikes.

So the picture is two sorts at once: lexical or helping (what the verb does) and finite or non-finite (what form it takes). Every verb in a sentence has one answer for each.

Non-finite verbs (verbals) have no tense or agreement of their own. They fill the later roles and the O, C and A slots.

1

Non-finite verbs: the three types

Non-finite verbs are verb forms with no tense, person or number of their own.

A non-finite verb cannot show tense or agree with a subject, and it cannot be the only verb of a sentence. It needs a finite verb, or a subject and context, to complete the sentence. She wants to leave. See the Finite tab for the full comparison.

The three types

TypeAlso calledTypical markerVerb formExamples
Infinitivebase verb formto + verb, or the bare verbV1to run, can run
Gerundverbal nounverb + -ing, works as a nounV4Swimming is fun.
Participleverbal adjectiveverb + -ing / -ed / -enV4 / V3a crying baby, a broken window

Key idea: each type keeps its verb qualities (it can take an object or an adverb) while also acting as another part of speech: a noun, an adjective or an adverb.

In the verb phrase, every role after Role 1 is non-finite. That is why been in has been waiting and waiting are slate, and only has is teal.

2

Infinitives

An infinitive is the base form of a verb, usually with to. It works as a noun, an adjective or an adverb.

Two basic kinds

KindFormExample
To-infinitive (full)to + base verbI want to learn.
Bare infinitive (plain)base verb, no toShe can swim.

Forms of the to-infinitive: aspect and voice

Negative and split to-infinitives

KindActivePassiveHow it works
Negativenot to writenot to be writtenput not before to
Splitto quickly writeto be carefully writtenan adverb between to and the verb

Bare infinitive: when to is dropped

  • After modal verbs (can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must): You must go.
  • After do, does, did (questions, negatives, emphasis): I did not see him.
  • After let, make and have (causative) + object: She made him laugh.
  • After perception verbs + object (see, hear, watch, notice, feel): I saw him leave. (the whole action)
  • After help (to is optional): He helped me (to) carry it.
  • After had better, would rather, would sooner, why not, why: You had better hurry.
  • After than and but / except when a bare verb comes before: He did nothing but complain.

Passive note: in the passive, make and perception verbs take to. He was made to apologise. He was seen to leave.

Special types

  • Split infinitive: an adverb sits between to and the verb (to boldly go). Fine in modern usage. Avoid it in very formal writing when a clearer alternative exists.
  • Infinitive of purpose: in order to / so as to + verb. She left early in order to catch the train.
  • Elliptical infinitive: the verb is dropped after to. Do you want to? Yes, I'd love to.
  • Semi-modals with to: ought to, have to, used to, be going to, be about to, be able to.
  • Emphatic use: All she did was (to) smile.
3

Gerunds and participles

Both can end in -ing. The job the word does tells you which one it is.

Gerunds (the -ing form as a noun)

A gerund keeps verb features: it can take an object and be modified by an adverb.

FormActivePassive
Simple (present)writingbeing written
Perfecthaving writtenhaving been written
Negativenot writingnot being written

Simple: same time as, or general with, the lexical verb. She enjoys singing. He hates being ignored. Perfect: earlier than the lexical verb. He denied having taken the money.

Function of the gerundExample
SubjectReading improves vocabulary.
Object of a verbShe enjoys painting.
Object of a prepositionHe is good at solving puzzles.
Subject complementMy hobby is collecting stamps.
AppositiveHis job, teaching, suits him.
Part of a compound nouna swimming pool, a washing machine
After a possessive or objectI dislike his leaving early. / I dislike him leaving early.
With go for activitiesLet's go shopping.

Possessive with a gerund: the possessive (his, Mary's) is more formal. The object form (him, Mary) is common in speech. When the gerund is the subject of the sentence, use the possessive: His leaving surprised us.

Fixed gerund expressions

  • It's no use / no good / not worth + gerund: It's no use crying.
  • can't help / can't stand / can't bear: I can't help laughing.
  • There's no + gerund: There's no denying it.   be worth: The film is worth watching.
  • be busy / spend / waste (time): She spent an hour cleaning.
  • Prepositional to + gerund (look forward to, be used to, get used to, object to, in addition to): I look forward to meeting you.
  • After other prepositions (in spite of, instead of, before, after, without, by, on): He left without saying goodbye.

Participial phrases

A participial phrase is a participle with its object and modifiers. It acts as an adjective or adverb. Its subject is understood to be the subject of the main clause.

  • Present: Opening the door, she saw a cat. (she opened and saw, nearly together)
  • Perfect: Having finished the work, he went home. (finished first)
  • Past: Delighted by the news, she smiled. (she was delighted)
  • Perfect passive: Having been warned twice, he still ignored the sign.
  • Negative: Not knowing the way, we asked for help.
  • Conjunction + participle: While waiting, he read. Although tired, she stayed.

Gerund vs present participle

TestGerundPresent participle

ExampleI like dancing.I saw her dancing.

Quick test: replace the -ing word with it. If the sentence still works, it is a gerund. I like it. works, so dancing is a gerund. I saw her it. does not work, so dancing is a participle.

Other useful patterns

  • Adjective + to-infinitive: happy, ready, eager, sorry, afraid, sure, likely, able, certain. She is eager to learn.
  • Noun + to-infinitive: chance, way, time, decision, ability, right, need. He had no time to waste.
  • Preposition + gerund: interested in, capable of, tired of, keen on, good at, afraid of, insist on. He is tired of waiting.
  • Sense verbs, two patterns: I saw him cross the road. (the whole action, bare infinitive) I saw him crossing the road. (in progress, participle)
5

Non-finite clauses

A non-finite clause is built around a non-finite verb and usually has no stated subject.

Clause typeExample
To-infinitive clauseTo finish on time, we worked late.
Bare infinitive clauseAll he did was cry.
-ing clauseHearing the news, she cried.
-ed clauseExhausted by the trip, he slept.

Absolute constructions

An absolute construction has its own subject, different from the subject of the main clause, and is not joined by a conjunction.

  • Noun + present participle: The weather being fine, we went out.
  • Noun + past participle: The work done, they went home.
  • Noun + perfect participle: The rain having stopped, we left.
  • With + noun + participle: With the sun setting, we turned back.
  • Fixed absolutes: weather permitting, all things considered, God willing, that said, generally speaking, judging by, granted that

Dangling (misrelated) participles

A participle must refer to the subject of the main clause. If it does not, it dangles.

Incorrect (dangling)Correct
Walking down the street, a car hit him.Walking down the street, he was hit by a car.
Having finished the work, the day was free.Having finished the work, she had a free day.
To pass the exam, hard work is needed.To pass the exam, you need hard work.

Exceptions: fixed phrases such as generally speaking, considering everything and judging by his looks are accepted as they stand.
