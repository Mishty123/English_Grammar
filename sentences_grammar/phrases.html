The Building Blocks of Sentences

Phrases

A phrase is a group of words that acts as a single part of speech. Unlike a clause, it does not contain a subject-verb pair.

On this page

    A phrase is a group of words that acts as a single part of speech. Unlike a clause, it does not contain a subject-verb pair.

    Quick check: does it have both a subject and a verb working together? If no, it's a phrase, not a clause.

    Phrase vs. Clause

    Phrase

    No subject-verb pair. Acts as a single part of speech (noun, verb, adjective, or adverb).

    Example: Running down the street

    Clause

    Has a subject and a verb together. May be independent (stands alone) or dependent (cannot stand alone).

    Example: …because he was running down the street

    Types of Phrases

    ten functional categories

    TypeFunctionBuilt aroundExamples
    Noun PhraseFunctions as a subject, object, or complement.noun + modifiersThe tall old man smiled.
    I bought a beautiful antique clock.
    Verb PhraseFunctions as the predicate; shows action or state.main verb + helping verb(s)She has been working all day.
    They will arrive soon.
    Adjective PhraseModifies a noun or pronoun.adjective + modifiersThe soup, very hot and spicy, burned my tongue.
    He seemed extremely confident.
    Adverb PhraseModifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb.adverb + modifiersHe drove very carefully down the hill.
    She sang quite beautifully.
    Prepositional PhraseActs as an adjective or adverb.preposition + objectThe book on the table is mine.
    She walked into the room.
    Gerund PhraseFunctions as a noun.verb+ing + modifiers/objectsSwimming in the ocean is fun.
    I enjoy reading mystery novels.
    Participial PhraseFunctions as an adjective.verb+ing / verb+ed + modifiersWalking down the street, I saw a dog.
    Exhausted from the trip, they slept.
    Infinitive PhraseFunctions as a noun, adjective, or adverb.to + base verbI want to learn English.
    She came to help me.
    Appositive PhraseRenames or explains a nearby noun.noun phrase beside a nounMy brother, a talented artist, painted this.
    Absolute PhraseModifies the whole sentence, not one word.noun + participleHer homework finished, she went outside.

    Combining Sentences with Phrases

    Three ways to fold one short sentence into another using a phrase instead of a clause.

    1. Using participial phrases

    Reduce one sentence into a descriptive opener or modifier.

    She waited by the door. She was tired.
    Combined: Tired, she waited by the door.

    2. Using appositive phrases

    Combine two sentences that describe the same noun.

    My brother is a talented artist. He painted this.
    Combined: My brother, a talented artist, painted this.

    3. Using prepositional phrases

    Add detail (place, time, or manner) without creating a new clause.

    The cat is sleeping. It is under the table.
    Combined: The cat is sleeping under the table.

    Common Mistakes

    ✗Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful. (Incorrect — a dangling modifier; it sounds like the trees were walking.) ✓Walking down the street, I thought the trees looked beautiful. (Correct — the phrase now clearly modifies “I.”)
    ✗She served sandwiches to the children wrapped in plastic. (Incorrect — a misplaced modifier; it sounds like the children were wrapped in plastic.) ✓She served sandwiches wrapped in plastic to the children. (Correct — the phrase now sits next to “sandwiches,” the word it modifies.)
    ✗My brother a talented artist painted this. (Incorrect — the appositive isn't set off, so the sentence is hard to parse.) ✓My brother, a talented artist, painted this. (Correct — commas mark the non-essential appositive phrase.)

    Remember

    • A phrase never has both a subject and a verb together — that's what separates it from a clause.
    • Phrases function as a single part of speech: noun, verb, adjective, or adverb.
    • Participial and absolute phrases must clearly connect to the word they modify, or they become dangling or misplaced modifiers.
    • Appositives that add extra, non-essential information are set off with commas on both sides.
    • Practice combining short sentences with phrases for smoother, more concise writing.
