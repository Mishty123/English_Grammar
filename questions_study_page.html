Question structures

Questions

In English, a question usually flips the subject and the helping (auxiliary) verb. The verb comes before the subject. Subject-Auxiliary Inversion is the name for this. A few exceptions keep the normal order.

How the pattern works

Three types of sentences, each with the verb in front of the subject.

With “to be” and modal verbs

When the main verb is to be (am, is, are, was, were), or the sentence already has a helping or modal verb (can, will, should, have), move that verb in front of the subject.

She is ready.
Is she ready?
They can swim.
Can they swim?
You have finished.
Have you finished?

With action verbs (using do / does / did)

If there is no helping verb, just a regular action verb in the simple present or simple past, English brings in do, does or did to sit in front of the subject. The main verb stays in its base form after the subject.

You like coffee.
Do you like coffee?
He works here.
Does he work here?
They arrived early.
Did they arrive early?

With question words (who, what, where, when, why, how)

Even when you start with a question word, the verb-before-subject order still applies right after it.

Where are you going? Why did she leave? What can I do?

More question patterns

Longer verb phrases, question phrases, negatives, small words at the end, and tags.

With more than one helping verb

When a verb phrase has two or three helpers (has been waiting, will be going, should have finished), only the very first helper moves in front of the subject. The rest stay after the subject.

She has been waiting.
Has she been waiting?
They will be arriving soon.
Will they be arriving soon?

Not “Has been she waiting?”

With question phrases (question word + noun, or how + word)

When a question word teams up with a noun (which book, what time), or how teams up with an adjective or adverb (how often, how many), the whole phrase stays together at the start, before the verb and subject flip.

You want which book.
Which book do you want?
She swims how often.
How often does she swim?

More on question phrases in the Question phrases tab.

Negative questions

Negative questions usually express surprise, or expect the listener to agree. The word order shifts slightly, depending on whether you use a contraction.

Don’t you like tea? Isn’t she here?
Do you not like tea? Is she not here?

Questions ending in a preposition (“small word”)

In everyday English, prepositions (to, for, with, about, at) stay at the very end of the question instead of moving to the front.

You are talking to someone.
Who are you talking to?
She is looking for her keys.
What is she looking for?

Questions with there

In a there is / there are sentence, there takes the subject spot. The verb flips in front of it, as in any other question.

There is some milk.
Is there any milk?
There are two seats.
Are there any seats?
There was a problem.
Was there a problem?
How many seats are there? Where is there a bank?

In questions and negatives, some usually changes to any: some milk becomes any milk.

Question tags

Instead of flipping the main sentence, a short mini-question is added to the end to check or confirm information. It copies the helping verb (or uses do / does / did) and flips from positive to negative, or negative to positive.

She is ready, isn’t she?
They can’t swim, can they?
You like coffee, don’t you?

The main exceptions

Two situations where the subject comes first and you do not flip.

Subject questions

When the question word itself (who or what) is the subject doing the action, the word order stays the same as a normal statement.

Who called you? What happened yesterday?
Which student called you? Whose dog barked? Which book fell off the shelf?

Compare with an object question, where the flip comes back: Who did you call? and Which student did you call?

Indirect (embedded) questions

When a question is tucked inside a polite phrase or a larger statement, it goes back to the standard subject + verb order.

Can you tell me where is the station?
Can you tell me where the station is?
I wonder what time it is.

Quick reminders and extras

Common mistakes, choice questions and short answers.

Watch out for common mistakes

Do you be happy?
Are you happy?
Does he works here?
Does he work here?

Never add do / does / did when to be is the main verb. Always reset the main verb to its base form after do / does / did.

Choice questions (or)

Use the standard helper + subject + verb order, and offer two options.

Do you want tea or coffee?

Short answers

Mirror the exact helping verb used in the question.

Is she ready?
Yes, she is.
Can they swim?
No, they can’t.

Question words and question phrases

A question word asks for one piece of information. It can stand alone, or team up with a noun or an adjective to make a phrase.

The question words

Each one asks for a different kind of information.

Who – a person Whom – a person (object, formal) Whose – the owner What – a thing, or open choice Which – a choice from a known group When – time Where – place Why – reason How – way, or amount, with a word after it

The whole phrase stays together

A question phrase moves to the front as one piece. Then the verb and subject flip.

You want which book.
Which book do you want?
She swims how often.
How often does she swim?
He is how old.
How old is he?

Never split the phrase: not “How do you want many books?”

Question word + noun

What, which and whose can be followed by a noun.

What + noun

Use what when the choices are open, and you do not have a set group in mind.

What time is it? What colour is your bag? What sport do you play?

Which + noun

Use which when the choice is from a group you both know.

Which book do you want? Which one is yours? Which road leads to the station?

What is open: What film do you like? (any film). Which is limited: Which film shall we watch? (from the list).

Whose + noun

Use whose to ask who owns something.

Whose bag is this? Whose keys are these? Whose turn is it?

What kind / type / sort of + noun

Use these to ask about the type of something.

What kind of music do you like? What type of car does he drive?

How + a word

How teams up with an adjective or an adverb to ask about an amount, a degree or a manner.

How many and how much

How many goes with countable nouns (things you can count). How much goes with uncountable nouns (things you cannot count).

There are how many apples.
How many apples are there?
We need how much milk.
How much milk do we need?

How much is also used for price: How much is this bag?

How + adjective

Ask about a measurement or a degree.

How old are you? How tall is she? How far is the station? How heavy is the box?

How + adverb

Ask how often, how well or how fast something happens.

How often do you swim? How well does he sing? How fast can it go? How soon will you arrive?

How long

How long asks about time or length.

How long does the trip take? (time) How long is the bridge? (length)

How come (informal)

How come means why, but the word order stays like a statement: no flip, and no do.

How come did you leave early?
How come you left early?

Compare: Why did you leave early?

Rules and mistakes

Subject or object phrases, and the errors to avoid.

Is the phrase the subject or the object?

If the phrase is the subject (the one doing the action), there is no flip. If it is the object, the flip comes back.

Which student called you? (subject: no flip)
Which student did you call? (object: flip)
How many people came? (subject: no flip)
How many people did you invite? (object: flip)

Common mistakes

How much apples are there?
How many apples are there?
Which time is it?
What time is it?
How is your name?
What is your name?
How old you are?
How old are you?

Match the word to the noun (many with countable, much with uncountable), and keep the verb before the subject.

How question tags work

A short mini-question at the end of a sentence, to check or confirm something.

The opposite rule

A positive statement gets a negative tag. A negative statement gets a positive tag.

She is ready,
Positive → isn’t she?
They can’t swim,
Negative → can they?

Copy the helper

If the statement has a helping verb or be, the tag copies it.

She is late,
isn’t she?
They have left,
haven’t they?
He can drive,
can’t he?

No helper? Use do / does / did

If the statement has only an action verb, the tag uses do, does or did, matching the tense.

You like coffee,
don’t you?
He works here,
doesn’t he?
They arrived early,
didn’t they?

The tag uses a pronoun

The tag never repeats the noun. It uses a pronoun that matches the subject.

Tom is late,
isn’t he?
The girls sing well,
don’t they?
This is yours,
isn’t it?
There is some milk,
isn’t there?

Everyone, someone and nobody take they: Everyone is here, aren’t they?

Special cases

Where the rules need a little care.

I am → aren’t I

Am I not is correct but very formal, and “amn’t I” is not used. Everyday English says aren’t I.

I am late, aren’t I?

Let’s and commands

After Let’s the tag is shall we. After a command, the tag is will you, won’t you or can you.

Let’s go, shall we? Open the door, will you? Don’t be late, will you?

Negative words count as negative

Words like never, hardly, nobody, nothing, no one make the statement negative, so the tag is positive.

She never smokes, does she? Nobody called, did they? There is nothing left, is there?

Have as a main verb

With have meaning “own”, American English uses do. British English often copies have.

You have a car, don’t you? (American) You have a car, haven’t you? (British)

Meaning, answers and mistakes

How the voice changes the meaning, how to answer, and what to avoid.

Voice: rising or falling

The voice on the tag changes what you mean.

She is ready, isn’t she?
She is ready, isn’t she.

Answering a tag question

Answer with the facts, not with the tag. The answer repeats the helper, as in any short answer.

She is ready, isn’t she?
Yes, she is.
She is ready, isn’t she?
No, she isn’t.
They can’t swim, can they?
No, they can’t.

Common mistakes

They are late, isn’t it?
aren’t they?
You like tea, aren’t you?
don’t you?
She can swim, doesn’t she?
can’t she?

Copy the same helper, and match the subject. Do not use isn’t it for everything.
