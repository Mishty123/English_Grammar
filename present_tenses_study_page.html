Four tenses, built one step at a time. Ask what you want to say, then pick the tense.

Quick checks

  • Finished time? yesterday, last week, in 2020 → Simple Past, not Perfect.
  • for = how long (5 years). since = when it started (2020).
  • State verbs (know, like, want, need) mostly don't take V4.

    State verbs describe a state, not an action. They are usually used in the simple form.

    • Thinking: know, believe, understand, remember, forget, doubt
    • Feeling: like, love, hate, want, need, prefer
    • Senses: see, hear, smell, taste, seem
    • Having: have (own), own, belong, contain, cost

    ✗ I am knowing the answer → ✓ I know the answer.

    Exception: when the meaning changes to an action, V4 is fine.

    VerbState (simple)Action (V4)
    haveI have a car. (own)I am having lunch. (eating)
    thinkI think it is good. (opinion)I am thinking about dinner.
    seeI see the problem. (understand)I am seeing the doctor. (meeting)
    tasteThe soup tastes good.She is tasting the soup.
    beHe is kind. (always)He is being silly. (acting that way now)

    Informal speech sometimes uses V4 for emphasis: “I am loving this song.” Avoid it in tests and formal writing.

  • Negative and question: Simple uses do / does. The others move the helper word.
  • Agreement: V5 for he / she / it. Every noun is 3rd person: one = he / she / it, more than one = they.
  • Passive: BE + V3 · BE + being + V3 · HAVE + been + V3.

Present Simple

The simple aspect sees an action as a whole. It is a fact, a habit or a fixed arrangement, not something you watch while it happens.

Form
+Subject + V1 (he / she / it: V5)
−Subject + do not / does not + V1
?Do / Does + Subject + V1?
Examples
+−?
I work.I don't work.Do I work?
She works.She doesn't work.Does she work?
They work.They don't work.Do they work?

Pronouns cover all three persons: 1st (I, we), 2nd (you), 3rd (he, she, it, they). Every noun is 3rd person: one noun = he / she / it, more than one noun = they.

Spelling: V5 (he / she / it)
  • most verbs add -s: work → works
  • after s, sh, ch, x, z, o add -es: watch → watches, go → goes
  • consonant + y → -ies: study → studies (play → plays)
  • irregular: have → has, be → is

When to use it

  • 1

    Habits and routines

    Things you do again and again. Dots repeat in the past and the future.

    • I brush my teeth every night.
    • She walks to school on weekdays.
  • 2

    General facts and truths

    True at any time, so the line has no start and no end.

    • The sun rises in the east.
    • Water boils at 100°C.
  • 3

    Permanent situations

    How things are for a long time.

    • He lives in Boston.
    • My dad works for a bank.
  • 4

    Feelings, thoughts, senses, owning

    State verbs describe how things are. They usually stay in the simple: like, love, know, want, need, believe, own, hear.

    • I know the answer.
    • She loves music.
  • 5

    Timetables and schedules

    A fixed time in the future, set by a timetable. The present tense is used for the future here.

    • The film starts at 8.
    • School finishes at 3 on Friday.
  • 6

    Instructions and directions

    You say it as the steps happen.

    • Take the second left.
    • You add the eggs, then you stir.
  • 7

    Stories, jokes, commentary, headlines

    Used to make past events feel live.

    • Messi passes to Silva. He shoots!
    • A man walks into a shop and asks for…
  • 8

    After when, if, before, as soon as

    For future events, the part after these words uses the Present Simple. “will” stays out of that part.

    • I’ll call you when I arrive.
    • If it rains, we’ll stay home.
Key words
alwaysusuallyoftensometimesrarelyneverevery dayon Mondaysonce a weekin the morning
Watch out

A time word is optional. After does / doesn’t the verb goes back to V1: ✗ She doesn’t works → ✓ She doesn’t work. Frequency words go before the main verb and after be: She never eats meat. She is always late.

Present Continuous

The continuous aspect sees an action in progress. It has started and it is not finished. It feels temporary and active.

Form
+Subject + am / is / are + V4
−Subject + am / is / are not + V4
?Am / Is / Are + Subject + V4?
Examples
+−?
I am working.I am not working.Am I working?
She is working.She isn't working.Is she working?
They are working.They aren't working.Are they working?
Spelling: V4 (-ing form)
  • most verbs add -ing: work → working
  • drop a silent e: make → making
  • double the last letter after a short vowel: run → running, sit → sitting
  • ie → ying: lie → lying

When to use it

  • 1

    Happening right now

    You can see or hear it at this moment.

    • Look! It is raining.
    • She is talking on the phone.
  • 2

    In progress around now

    The action started and is not finished. It may not be happening this second.

    • I am reading a good book.
    • We are learning to swim.
  • 3

    Temporary situations

    It is true for a short time only.

    • He is staying with us this week.
    • I am working from home today.
  • 4

    Changing and developing

    Something is growing, falling or improving.

    • The weather is getting colder.
    • Your English is improving.
  • 5

    Trends

    A current movement in the world.

    • More people are working online.
    • Prices are going up.
  • 6

    Fixed future plans

    An arrangement made already, usually with a time.

    • We are flying to Rome on Friday.
    • I am meeting Anna at 6.
  • 7

    Annoying repeated habits with always

    The action repeats and it bothers the speaker.

    • He is always leaving his socks on the floor.
    • You are always losing your keys.
Key words
nowright nowat the momenttodaythis weekthese dayscurrentlyLook!Listen!
Watch out

State verbs are usually not used in the continuous: know, like, love, want, need, believe, understand. ✗ I am knowing → ✓ I know. Some verbs have two meanings: I have a car (own) but I am having lunch (eating).

Present Perfect

The perfect aspect links the past to now. The time is unfinished or not important. What matters is the result, the experience or the count.

Form
+Subject + have / has + V3
−Subject + haven't / hasn't + V3
?Have / Has + Subject + V3?
Examples
+−?
I have seen it.I haven't seen it.Have I seen it?
She has seen it.She hasn't seen it.Has she seen it?
They have seen it.They haven't seen it.Have they seen it?
Spelling: V3 (past participle)
  • regular verbs add -ed: work → worked
  • irregular verbs must be learned: go → gone, see → seen, eat → eaten, write → written, take → taken, do → done, be → been, have → had, make → made, buy → bought

When to use it

  • 1

    Life experience

    At some time before now. The exact time does not matter.

    • I have been to Japan.
    • Have you ever eaten sushi?
  • 2

    Recent action, result now

    The action is over, but the result is here now.

    • I have broken my cup. (it is broken now)
    • She has lost her phone.
  • 3

    Just happened, news

    Breaking news and very recent events.

    • The mayor has resigned.
    • Rita has just arrived.
  • 4

    Done or not done yet

    Already and yet show whether something is finished.

    • She has already left.
    • I haven't finished yet.
  • 5

    Unfinished time period

    The time word is still running: today, this week, this year.

    • I have drunk three coffees today.
    • He has called twice this week.
  • 6

    States that continue, with for / since

    Started in the past and still true now. Use state verbs here.

    • We have lived here since 2019.
    • She has known him for ten years.
  • 7

    Change over time

    Compare then and now.

    • My son has grown a lot.
    • Prices have gone up.
  • 8

    First time, best ever

    After “the first time” and superlatives.

    • It’s the first time I have flown.
    • This is the best pizza I have ever eaten.
Key words
everneverbeforealreadyjustyetso farrecentlylatelythis weektodayforsince
Watch out

Never use a finished time. ✗ I have seen him yesterday → ✓ I saw him yesterday. She has been to Paris = she went and came back. She has gone to Paris = she is still there.

Present Perfect Continuous

This joins the perfect and the continuous. It shows an activity that has been going on up to now. It focuses on the activity and how long, not on the result.

Form
+Subject + have / has been + V4
−Subject + haven't / hasn't been + V4
?Have / Has + Subject + been + V4?
Examples
+−?
I have been working.I haven't been working.Have I been working?
She has been working.She hasn't been working.Has she been working?
They have been working.They haven't been working.Have they been working?
Spelling: V4 (-ing form)
  • most verbs add -ing: work → working
  • drop a silent e: make → making
  • double the last letter after a short vowel: run → running, sit → sitting
  • ie → ying: lie → lying

When to use it

  • 1

    Started before, still going

    The activity started in the past and continues now.

    • I have been waiting for an hour.
    • It has been raining since morning.
  • 2

    How long?

    The question for the length of an activity.

    • How long have you been learning English?
    • How long has he been working here?
  • 3

    Just stopped, the proof is visible

    The activity ended a moment ago. You can see the effect now.

    • She has been crying. Her eyes are red.
    • Why are your hands wet? I have been washing the car.
  • 4

    Repeated activity lately

    Short activities that kept happening in a recent period.

    • He has been going to the gym a lot lately.
    • I have been eating more fruit recently.
  • 5

    Activity, not result

    Keeps the focus on the doing. The job may not be finished.

    • I have been painting the wall. (maybe not done)
    • They have been building the bridge for months.
  • 6

    Annoyance or criticism

    Often with a question or a complaint.

    • Who has been using my pen?
    • You have been ignoring me all day.
Key words
forsinceall dayall morningall weekhow longlatelyrecently
Watch out

State verbs usually do not take the continuous. ✗ I have been knowing her → ✓ I have known her for years. Count or finished result → Perfect Simple (I have written three emails). Activity or length → Perfect Continuous (I have been writing all morning).

6

Perfect Simple vs Continuous

B2

Result or count, against activity or duration.

IdeaPerfect SimplePerfect Continuous
How many or how longHow many pages have you read?How long have you been reading?
Result or activityI have painted the room. (it is done)I have been painting the room. (the activity, maybe not done)
Count or durationI have written three emails.I have been writing emails all morning.
State verbsI have known her for years.✗ I have been knowing her.

Both can use for / since.

7

Present Perfect vs Simple Past

B1

The time decides. Finished time → Simple Past. Unfinished or not stated → Present Perfect.

IdeaPresent PerfectSimple Past
The timeUnfinished or not statedFinished, said or understood
A result nowI have lost my keys. (I still cannot find them)I lost my keys yesterday.
Experience or a momentHave you ever seen it?Did you see it last night?
Still true or notI have lived here for 5 years. (I still live here)I lived there for 5 years. (I do not now)
This or lastI have been busy this week.I was busy last week.

Watch outFinished time words need the Simple Past: yesterday, last week, in 2020, two days ago. Unfinished time words go with the Present Perfect: today, this week, so far.

8

For and Since

B1–B2

They show how long. They work with the Present Perfect and the Present Perfect Continuous.

for + a length of timesince + a starting point
Meaninghow long it has lastedwhen it started
Examplesfor 5 years, for two hours, for a long time, for a whilesince 2020, since Monday, since 10 a.m., since I was ten
SimpleI have lived here for 5 years.I have lived here since 2020.
ContinuousShe has been studying for 3 hours.She has been studying since morning.
NegativeThey haven't seen him for a while.They haven't seen him since Monday.

Quick test: if you can count it, use for. If you can point to it on a clock or a calendar, use since. Do not use “ago” with the Present Perfect.

10

Present Tenses in the Passive

B2–C1

The subject receives the action. The action or the result matters more than who did it.

Simple→ BE + V3
Continuous→ BE + BEING + V3
Perfect→ HAVE + BEEN + V3
Tense and pattern+ and −?
Present Simple
Subject + am / is / are + V3		The book is read.
The book isn't read.		Is the book read?
Present Continuous
Subject + am / is / are being + V3		The road is being repaired.
The road isn't being repaired.		Is the road being repaired?
Present Perfect
Subject + have / has been + V3		The letter has been sent.
The letter hasn't been sent.		Has the letter been sent?
When to use
  • The action matters, not the doerThe bridge is being repaired.
  • The doer is unknown or unimportantMy bike has been stolen.
  • Formal writing, news, instructionsThe form must be signed. Tickets are sold online.
  • Name the doer only when it mattersThe letter has been sent by the manager.

Active → passive: She writes a letter. → A letter is written by her. The object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive sentence.
